# History of Oric

An introduction to the Oric home computers for readers who know the C64 but not the Oric: where the machines came from,
how they sold, what became of the company and who keeps them running today. The hardware itself is described in the
next appendix, [Oric Tech Specs](oric_specs.md).

The history comes from the English and French Wikipedia articles and the other sources under
[Further Reading](#further-reading). Where they disagree, as they often do on dates and sales, both figures are given.
Both Wikipedia articles are flagged as short of citations, so treat exact numbers as approximate.

---

## Tangerine and the Microtan (1979–1982)

The Oric began at Tangerine Computer Systems, founded in 1979 in St Ives, Cambridgeshire, by Dr Paul Johnson, Mark
Rainer and Nigel Penton Tilbury. Its first product was the TAN1648, a VDU kit, and its first computer the Microtan 65:
one of the first 6502 kit computers, built as rack-mounted boards with a little RAM, a character generator and a UHF
modulator, and expandable with a serial port, more memory and BASIC in ROM. A later design, the Microtan 2, was sold to
the amplifier maker HH Electronics, which released it as the HH Tiger without much success. Tangerine also made Prestel
adapters under the Tantel name.

Rainer and Penton Tilbury stepped back to consultancy work, Barry Muncaster joined the running of the company, and it
moved to Ely. After the success of Sinclair's ZX Spectrum, Tangerine's backers suggested a cheap colour home computer.
Tangerine set up Oric Products International in April 1982 to build one around the same 6502, and Tansoft (from
"Tangerine Software") became its software label.

---

## The Oric-1 (1982–1983)

The Oric-1 went on sale in the UK in the autumn of 1982 (the English Wikipedia gives 1 September; other sources say
1983). It cost £129 with 16K of RAM and £169 with 48K, a few pounds under the 48K Spectrum, and offered what the
Spectrum lacked: a proper sound chip, a printer port, and colour that did not clash in the Spectrum's way, though it
had limits of its own.

It sold well. About 160,000 went in the UK in 1983, short of the 350,000 the company had predicted, and about 50,000 in
France, where it was the best-selling home computer that year and was voted *ordinateur de l'année* (computer of the
year) for 1983. It launched there at 2,000 FF and later fell to 1,000 FF.

The weak points were the small rubbery keyboard, the tape loader and the first ROM. BASIC 1.0's tape verify routine was
faulty, so programs saved by users could fail to load again, and several other bugs were left for the next ROM.

On 13 October 1983 a fire at the Kenure Plastics factory in Berkshire, where the machines were made, destroyed about
7,000 Orics. Production moved and restarted quickly; Personal Computer News reported it under the headline "Oric lit
up".

## Edenspring and the Atmos (1984)

Late in 1983 Edenspring Investments bought the company and put £4m into it. The result was the Oric Atmos, launched in
February 1984: the same board in a black and red case with a proper keyboard, and the BASIC 1.1 ROM, which fixed most
of 1.0's bugs but not the tape verify. 1.1 also moved some ROM routines, so not every Oric-1 program runs on an Atmos,
and many tapes carried a 1.0 version on one side and a 1.1 version on the other. Oric-1 owners could fit the new ROM in
a spare socket with a switch between the two.

The Atmos was France's computer of the year for 1984, and fell there from 1,990 FF to 990 FF. The French article counts
27,000 Atmos sold between February and June 1984, and an installed base in France of about 120,000.

The peripherals promised since the Oric-1 arrived during 1984:

| Peripheral         | What it was                                                |
|:-------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Microdisc          | 3-inch floppy drive, summer 1984; about 3,600 FF in France |
| MCP-40             | Four-colour pen plotter and printer                        |
| Modem              | Released alongside the drive and printer                   |
| Prestel adaptor    | The first Oric videotext adaptor, with little software     |

The Microdisc had been announced for the autumn of 1983 and arrived nearly a year late, at more than three times the
price of the computer. Its ROM can be switched out, which makes the 16K of RAM under the BASIC ROM usable by the disk
operating system. The MCP-40's mechanism was made by Alps and shared with the Atari 1020, the Commodore 1520 and others.

## The Stratos and the Receivers (1985)

Early in 1985 Oric announced several new models, among them IBM-compatible and MSX-compatible machines. On 1 February
it showed the Oric Stratos (also called the IQ164) at the Frankfurt computer show. The next day, 2 February, Edenspring
put Oric International into receivership.

The French company Eureka Informatique took over the French business on 1 June 1985 and carried on with the Stratos
design. In November 1985 Eureka released SEDORIC, a disk operating system by Fabrice Broche and Denis Sebbag, which
became the standard DOS for Oric disk users.

---

## Eureka and the Telestrat (1986–1987)

Eureka's Stratos became the Oric Telestrat, launched in France in April 1986 at 3,990 FF. It kept the Atmos's CPU,
graphics and sound, had 64K of RAM, a disk drive as standard and more ports, and was aimed at Minitel, the French
videotext network, with a Minitel port and telematic software built in. Atmos software ran through a cartridge, and a
compiled BASIC, Hyper-BASIC, came on another. About 6,000 were sold, all in France.

Eureka announced a Telestrat 2 in September 1987, then went into receivership in December 1987, the second and final
end of Oric. The French article says remaining Telestrats were sold until December 1988.

## Clones

The Atmos design outlived the company in Eastern Europe:

| Clone      | Country    | Differences                                                 |
|:-----------|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------------------|
| Pravetz 8D | Bulgaria   | 1985–1991; white case, mechanical keys, internal supply     |
| Nova 64    | Yugoslavia | Licensed Atmos; only the logo changed                       |

The Pravetz 8D's patched ROM gives Latin letters in upper case and Cyrillic in lower case, with a Caps Lock key to
switch. In 1987–88 Borislav Zahariev added an Apple Disk II compatible interface and a DOS, DOS-8D.

## Software, Magazines and Sales

The Oric.org archive counts 589 commercially released games from 1983 to 1987. The early titles were mostly British:
Tansoft, IJK, PSS, Level 9, Microdeal, Artic and others. From 1984 the market and the software became French:
Loriciels, ERE Informatique, Cobra Soft, Infogrames, Micropuce, Norsoft and many smaller houses wrote for the Oric
long after British publishers had moved on.

The magazines followed the same path, from Britain's Oric User Monthly to France's Théoric and Micro'Oric, and later
CEO-Mag, the Club Europe Oric's magazine, which is still published.

Totals are uncertain. The English article gives 210,000 Oric-1s, most of them in 1983; the French article gives a little
under 400,000 Orics of all models across Europe.

## The Oric Today

The Oric has one of the most active communities of any 8-bit machine, centred in France and Britain. The Club Europe
Oric (CEO) publishes CEO-Mag and runs [oric.org](https://www.oric.org/), the software archive, with its forum at
[forums.oric.org](https://forums.oric.org/). [Defence Force](https://www.defence-force.org/computing/oric/index.htm)
holds manuals, hardware notes and new software.

Emulation began with Fabrice Francès's Euphoric for MS-DOS. Today Oricutron (Peter Gordon), Clock Signal (Tom Harte)
and Auric (Anders Piniesjö) run the original tapes and disks; the tech specs appendix has notes on running Auric.

New hardware keeps real machines in use: Erebus replaces the tape deck with an SD card of `.tap` files, Cumulus
replaces the Microdisc with SD card disk images, and the Twilighte board adds 512K of RAM, an EEPROM, USB and SD card
loading and joystick ports. LOCI, a newer add-on, has its own file manager and software on oric.org.
